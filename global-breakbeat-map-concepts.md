# Global Breakbeat Map: concept directions

## Diagnosis of the rejected draft

The first draft tried to be a map, timeline, family tree and glossary at the same time. Geography became a background for labelled cards, the arrows implied relationships more confidently than the article could prove, and there was no single reading order. The next version needs one editorial point that is understandable in five seconds.

## Concept 1: Breakbeat Across the Atlantic

**Single idea:** one broken rhythmic language became several distinct local club cultures.

```text
BREAKBEAT ACROSS THE ATLANTIC
How five places made broken rhythm local

  WEST COAST      NEW YORK          UK          ANDALUSIA
      ●               ◎              ●              ●
       \             source          \              /
        \                            FLORIDA ●

◎ source method   ● local scene   thin line = circulation, not ownership
```

The map itself occupies about 75% of the canvas. New York is identified as the historical DJ-method source, while the UK, Florida, the US West Coast and Andalusia are treated as locally organised scenes rather than derivative destinations. Each place gets only a date range and one short descriptor:

- New York, 1970s: DJs extend the break.
- UK, 1988–2000s: rave accelerates and branches.
- Florida, early 1990s onward: bass cultures form a regional scene.
- US West Coast, 1990s: electro, acid and rave remain porous.
- Andalusia, 1992–2000s: breaks become mass regional culture.

**Included:** real geography, five places, restrained dates, one claim per place, a tiny legend and only historically defensible circulation lines.

**Excluded:** artist lists, BPM, subgenre taxonomies, club names, paragraphs, decorative transport routes and claims that one region “invented” every other scene.

**Why people may share or cite it:** it gives readers one useful correction to the usual UK-only story: the same rhythmic idea was organised differently by several local cultures.

**Weakness:** the word “global” would overstate a map centred on Atlantic histories. `Breakbeat Across the Atlantic` is the more credible title; a caption can state that these are selected hubs, not a complete world census.

## Concept 2: Four Local Scenes, One Shared Vocabulary

**Single idea:** compare how four regions turned shared breakbeat techniques into different scene identities.

```text
FOUR LOCAL SCENES, ONE SHARED VOCABULARY

 [UK map crop]        [Florida map crop]
 rave + hardcore      electro + Miami bass

 [West Coast crop]    [Andalusia map crop]
 acid + electro       regional rave culture

        [small world locator with four dots]
```

This is a small-multiples atlas rather than a single route map. Four equally sized geographic plates show the UK, central Florida, the US West Coast and Andalusia. A small locator map establishes their global position. New York appears in a short root note above the plates rather than as a fifth competing scene card.

**Included:** four true map crops, one city or regional anchor per plate, approximate emergence dates, one concise scene description and a shared visual scale where practical.

**Excluded:** arrows, origin-to-destination narratives, artist lists, genre family trees and any suggestion that all four regions developed simultaneously or identically.

**Why people may share or cite it:** the side-by-side structure makes regional comparison easy and can be reused as an editorial reference when discussing why breakbeat never meant exactly the same thing everywhere.

**Weakness:** it reads more like a magazine spread than a conventional world map. On mobile the four plates must become a vertical sequence, reducing the instant global overview.

## Concept 3: Records Travel, Scenes Stay Local

**Single idea:** records and DJs circulated internationally, but scenes were built by local rooms, labels, radio and audiences.

```text
RECORDS TRAVEL, SCENES STAY LOCAL

        [single Atlantic-centred map]

   ● West Coast  ···  ● Florida  ···  ◎ New York
          ····························
                         ● UK  ···  ● Andalusia

 dotted arcs = documented circulation
 solid local rings = scene infrastructure
```

The geography remains dominant, but the visual grammar contrasts thin dotted international circulation arcs with solid rings around local hubs. The rings are the central device: they represent the local infrastructure that made a scene durable. No region is presented as a simple endpoint.

**Included:** five geographic anchors, two line types, restrained time labels and one legend that explains circulation versus local infrastructure.

**Excluded:** speculative one-way influence arrows, exhaustive touring routes, detailed venue networks, decorative planes or ships and subgenre boxes.

**Why people may share or cite it:** the graphic communicates a stronger thesis than a normal influence map: music travels, but scenes are social systems built locally.

**Weakness:** every drawn route needs documentary support. If the evidence is uneven, the dotted lines can look authoritative while actually being selective.

## Recommendation

Proceed with **Concept 1: Breakbeat Across the Atlantic**.

- **Five-second comprehension:** strongest. One map, five anchors, one sentence.
- **Editorial credibility:** it distinguishes a source method from later local scenes and avoids a false family-tree hierarchy.
- **Backlink potential:** the image makes a clear, quotable claim without trying to replace the article.
- **Desktop and mobile:** the desktop version can use the full Atlantic field; mobile can use the same hierarchy as a vertical map crop with external labels, not a different infographic.

Before production, approve the concept and the more accurate title `Breakbeat Across the Atlantic`. No new SVG should be drawn until that choice is confirmed.
