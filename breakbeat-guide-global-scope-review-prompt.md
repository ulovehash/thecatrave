# Breakbeat Guide Global-Scope Review Prompt

You are a senior electronic-music editor, music historian and technical SEO strategist reviewing a major rewrite of an existing article on thecatrave.com.

The old page used the H1:

> **Evolution of Breakbeat Music in the UK and Its Modern Legacy**

The new page is intended to answer a broader primary intent:

> **breakbeat music as a genre, connected family of scenes and international club-music history**

The proposed new H1 is:

> **What Is Breakbeat? A Guide to the Genre, History and Styles**

The public URL and canonical must remain unchanged:

- `/breakbeat-guide`
- `https://thecatrave.com/breakbeat-guide`

Your job is not to assume that changing the H1 has successfully changed the article's scope. Audit whether the rewritten article is genuinely about breakbeat music as a whole or whether it is still, underneath the new metadata, an article about the evolution of breakbeat in the UK.

## Central editorial question

Determine whether the page now delivers the history and meaning promised by the new H1.

The article may give the UK substantial space because British rave, breakbeat hardcore, big beat and nu-skool breaks are central to the story. Equal word counts for every country are not required. The problem is not UK detail; the problem would be treating a British chronology as the whole genre while reducing every other history to a definition, footnote or artist list.

Judge scope through narrative function, not geography quotas.

## What the article should cover

The article's main subject is breakbeat as music, genre and scene history. It is not primarily:

- a UK electronic-music timeline;
- a guide to programming broken drums;
- a general history of hip-hop;
- a general history of jungle or drum and bass;
- a list of every genre that uses sampled breaks;
- an encyclopedia of every regional breakbeat scene.

A convincing broad history should normally explain:

1. the source-breaks in funk and soul;
2. Bronx hip-hop DJ practice and the transformation of the break into a method;
3. sampling technology only where it changes the historical possibilities;
4. the transition from rhythmic method into electronic dance and club genres;
5. UK rave and breakbeat hardcore;
6. the branching relationship with jungle and drum and bass without absorbing those cultures into breakbeat;
7. Florida / Orlando breaks as a distinct American regional history;
8. big beat as a related crossover branch;
9. nu-skool breaks and the dedicated late-1990s/2000s breaks circuit;
10. contemporary breaks and breakbeat techno without claiming one unified global revival;
11. the difference between breakbeat, breaks, big beat, jungle, drum and bass, broken beat and breakcore;
12. immediately playable examples that make the distinctions audible.

Additional regions such as California, Spain/Andalusia, Australia or other local scenes should be added only if reliable evidence shows that they materially improve the main history. Do not demand token paragraphs merely to make the article look international.

## 1. Audit the hidden legacy of the old UK article

Look for evidence that the old H1 still controls the new draft, including:

- a chronology whose only real narrative is British;
- American origins treated merely as a preface to the UK story;
- Florida breaks named in metadata and headings but given no scene history;
- international artists appearing only in lists;
- a conclusion that treats current UK club music as the entire present;
- repeated internal links or language that frame the page as “UK breakbeat evolution”;
- headings inherited from the old article that no longer match the broader intent;
- a title/H1 promise that is broader than the body copy;
- British examples presented as universal definitions;
- old UK-ranking phrases preserved so aggressively that they distort the new article.

For every problem found, distinguish between:

- useful UK authority that should be protected;
- UK material that needs reframing;
- UK material that is repetitive or disproportionate;
- a genuinely missing non-UK scene or transition.

Do not remove strong UK material merely to manufacture symmetry.

## 2. Search-intent and SEO migration

The page previously ranked with a UK-focused H1. The new goal is to broaden relevance without losing earned authority.

If Search Console or Ahrefs exports are supplied, use them to identify:

- the current dominant query intent;
- high-impression UK formulations that should remain naturally represented;
- broader definition and genre queries the new article should satisfy;
- supporting queries for Florida breaks, nu-skool breaks, breakbeat hardcore, breakbeat artists, breakbeat styles and comparisons;
- queries that belong to a separate production/rhythm article rather than this page;
- wording that should be retained in body copy or an H2 even if it no longer belongs in the H1.

Do not invent query volume, rankings or competitor findings when data is absent. Label every conclusion as one of:

- supported by supplied first-party search data;
- supported by competitor evidence;
- editorial inference requiring validation.

Do not recommend:

- changing the URL or canonical;
- keyword stuffing;
- repeating “breakbeat music” mechanically;
- making the page longer solely to rank;
- combining unrelated intents such as `electronic music genres`;
- deleting historically useful UK sections just because the H1 is now broader.

## 3. Historical and geographical balance

Create a map of the article's actual narrative attention. For each major scene or period, record:

- approximate space and number of subsections;
- whether it has causes, places, people, infrastructure and representative records;
- whether it advances the main breakbeat history;
- whether it receives a playable example;
- whether it is treated as a living culture or only a genre definition.

At minimum, compare:

- funk/soul source records;
- Bronx hip-hop DJ culture;
- UK rave and breakbeat hardcore;
- Florida/Orlando breaks;
- big beat;
- nu-skool breaks;
- contemporary breaks and breakbeat techno.

The correct result is not equal coverage. The correct result is proportionate coverage that explains why each branch matters.

## 4. Competitor comparison

Compare the draft with the strongest pages ranking for relevant breakbeat queries. Judge whether competitors cover:

- the rhythm/genre distinction;
- origins and sampler history;
- UK rave;
- Florida breaks;
- big beat and nu-skool breaks;
- artists and essential tracks;
- contemporary breaks;
- genre comparisons;
- interactive listening;
- maps, timelines, flyers, record sleeves and scene artifacts.

Identify only consequential gaps. Do not copy a competitor's structure merely because it ranks. Explain whether a missing element improves reader understanding or merely increases topical breadth.

## 5. Reader-experience test

Read the article as three people:

1. a newcomer searching “what is breakbeat music?”;
2. a knowledgeable listener who will notice weak genre boundaries;
3. a breaks fan from outside the UK who wants their history treated seriously.

For each reader, answer:

- Does the opening make the scope clear?
- Does the chronology feel coherent?
- Is the UK emphasis justified rather than assumed?
- Can the reader hear each major branch immediately?
- Does the article explain where scenes happened and how they circulated?
- Does the ending describe a genuinely broader present?

## 6. Heading and metadata integrity

Check whether the following agree with one another:

- SEO title;
- meta description;
- H1;
- introduction;
- H2 sequence;
- conclusion;
- FAQ;
- internal links and anchor text.

The H1 may remain “What Is Breakbeat? A Guide to the Genre, History and Styles” only if the article actually fulfils that broad promise.

If a heading materially preserves the old UK-only framing, provide an exact replacement. Do not rewrite headings that are already clear and useful.

## 7. Content-preservation test

Protect the elements that likely helped the previous page rank or earn links:

- unchanged URL and canonical;
- accurate UK evolution material;
- important artist, label, club and track entities;
- legacy fragment anchors;
- natural internal links;
- useful comparison and FAQ formulations;
- any wording supported by Search Console impressions;
- the strongest cultural and historical passages from the old article.

Flag anything removed from the old article that appears to have search value or external-link value. Recommend whether it should be:

- restored in concise form;
- moved to another article;
- represented by a preserved anchor;
- safely omitted.

Do not promise that rankings cannot move. Evaluate whether the migration is careful, defensible and measurable.
