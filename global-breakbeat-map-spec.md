# The Global Breakbeat Map

## Editorial purpose

Create an original, branded visual reference that explains how a shared break-based musical language became several different local club cultures. The map must work in three ways:

1. orient readers before the article moves into regional history;
2. remain useful when shared without the surrounding article;
3. give writers, educators and music sites a reason to cite and embed the original guide.

The map is not a claim that breakbeat existed only in the places shown. It presents selected, well-documented regional histories covered by this article.

## Recommended public title

**The Global Breakbeat Map**

**Four regional scenes that turned broken rhythms into local club culture.**

Permanent qualification inside the graphic:

> Selected major regional histories covered in this guide. Not an exhaustive map of every breakbeat scene.

## Core editorial model

The visual contains one source-practice node and four regional routes:

- **Source practice:** New York / South Bronx in the 1970s.
- **Regional route 1:** United Kingdom from the late 1980s into the 2000s.
- **Regional route 2:** Central Florida from the Orlando Summer of Love into the wider 1990s scene.
- **Regional route 3:** US West Coast during the 1990s and early 2000s.
- **Regional route 4:** Andalusia, with a documented central arc from 1992 to 2002 and a continuing regional identity.

The source-practice node must not be labelled as an early electronic breakbeat scene. It explains how DJs extended funk and soul drum breaks and how that method entered hip-hop and sampling culture.

## Final map copy

### ROOTS / NEW YORK

**South Bronx · 1970s**

Hip-hop DJs extended the drum breaks in funk and soul records for dancers. The break became reusable musical material before breakbeat became a club-genre name.

**Key reference:** Kool Herc and break-based DJ practice.

### UNITED KINGDOM

**Late 1980s–2000s · nationwide rave network, London and Brighton nodes**

UK hip-hop, acid house, techno, reggae, dub and rave collided in breakbeat hardcore. The route later opened into jungle, big beat and the dedicated nu-skool breaks circuit.

**Infrastructure:** warehouses, clubs, pirate radio, white labels, record shops and specialist nights.

**Reference names:** Shut Up and Dance, SL2, 2 Bad Mice, The Prodigy; later the Chemical Brothers, Adam Freeland, Rennie Pilgrem, Plump DJs and Stanton Warriors.

### CENTRAL FLORIDA

**Orlando · 1989–mid-1990s formation, continuing legacy**

Miami bass, freestyle, electro, hip-hop, house and imported UK records became a bass-heavy regional breaks identity rather than a copy of one British sound.

**Infrastructure:** Beacham Theatre / AAHZ, The Edge, Underground Record Source and Zone Records.

**Reference names:** Kimball Collins, Dave Cannalte, DJ Stylus, DJ Icey and DJ Baby Anne.

### US WEST COAST

**San Francisco, Los Angeles and Orange County · 1990s–early 2000s**

An open rave network moved between electro, hip-hop, house, acid, psychedelia and British imports. “West Coast breaks” remained a useful DJ and record-bin identity rather than one fixed formula.

**Infrastructure:** Bay Area rave networks, Los Angeles radio and club circulation, independent labels and touring DJs.

**Reference names:** Bassbin Twins, Überzone and Simply Jeff. Hardkiss appears as wider San Francisco rave context, not as proof of a single breaks genre.

### ANDALUSIA

**Seville, Málaga, Cádiz, Huelva and Granada · 1992–2002 central arc, continuing identity**

British records, tourism, local DJs and large outdoor events helped turn breakbeat from an import into mass regional youth culture with unusual scale and longevity.

**Infrastructure:** record shops, regional promoters, sports halls and open-air events.

**Historical marker:** Expo '92 begins the documentary's central timeline; *Break Nation* records the scene's collective history.

### NOW / GLOBAL CIRCULATION

Breaks continue as a named scene while broken drums circulate through electro, garage, techno, bass music, jungle and contemporary rave. This is a circulation layer, not a fifth unified regional scene.

## Connections shown on the map

The map must avoid a simplistic `New York → UK → rest of world` chain.

- A black root line connects break-based DJ and sampling practice to the shared rhythmic vocabulary.
- Coloured routes then separate into UK, Central Florida, West Coast and Andalusia.
- A solid UK–Andalusia line represents imported records, visiting artists and tourism.
- A solid UK–Florida line represents imported UK records inside the Orlando mixture.
- A dotted Florida–West Coast line represents US touring, records and overlapping breaks circuits without claiming one scene produced the other.
- All four routes taper into a neutral contemporary circulation band rather than merging into one genre.

Legend:

- **Solid line:** documented circulation or strong historical influence.
- **Dotted line:** exchange, touring or scene overlap.
- **Colour:** local route, not ownership of a genre.

## Mobile composition

Do not shrink the landscape graphic.

Use a portrait version with this order:

1. title and qualification;
2. New York source-practice card;
3. simplified Atlantic map with four coloured destinations;
4. UK card;
5. Central Florida card;
6. West Coast card;
7. Andalusia card;

The mobile graphic should be a separate art-directed asset, not a horizontal SVG inside an overflow container.

## Placement in the article

Replace the current visual inside the section with `id="history-map"`, while preserving that anchor.

Recommended article sequence:

1. origins in funk, hip-hop and sampling;
2. short paragraph explaining that breakbeat did not travel as one finished genre;
3. **The Global Breakbeat Map**;
4. regional history sections in the same order as the map;
5. listening examples distributed through those sections.

The current lineage diagram should be retained as source material for the later **Breakbeat Family Tree** rather than deleted conceptually.

## Branding system

- Keep the wordmark small and editorial: `thecatrave*`.
- Include the canonical URL and update year.
- Use the article palette: paper, black, cyan, coral and yellow; introduce at most one additional colour for Andalusia.
- Do not use a central watermark.
- Do not place long citations inside the map. Keep source links in the article and downloadable metadata/readme.

## Claims intentionally excluded from the first version

- A claim that any one regional scene invented breakbeat as a whole.
- A complete map of every city or country where breaks were played.
- Precise arrows between scenes without documentary support.
- A claim that Hardkiss represents a unified West Coast breaks genre.
- A claim that the Andalusian scene ended in 2002.
- Artist rankings such as “most important” or “first” unless strongly sourced.

## Approval and QA checklist

- Every date, place, artist and institution shown has at least one supporting source.
- Lines describe documented circulation or are explicitly labelled as overlap.
- The graphic remains readable at article width on desktop.
- The mobile asset is a separate portrait composition.
- The map still makes sense without the article.
- The article retains crawlable text and does not rely on the image for factual coverage.
- Branding is visible but does not dominate the information.
- Download and embed links point back to the canonical guide.

## Measurement

Track full-size opens, PNG/SVG downloads, embed-code reveals and referring domains. Review Google Images impressions and image-result clicks after indexing. If readers use the map but do not embed or cite it, the next iteration should make the regional data more useful rather than increasing the branding.
